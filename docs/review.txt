-------------------- Review 1 --------------------

Overall Recommendation: 5 (5 - marginal - only just acceptable)
Evaluation Confidence: 4 (4 - Pretty confident, I know this area well)

Summary: This paper proposes OUGS, a framework for object-centric active view selection in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS).
The key idea is to model uncertainty directly from the physical parameters of the Gaussians (position, scale, rotation, color), propagate it to pixel space through the rendering Jacobian, and combine it with a semantic probability mask (from SAM-2) to produce an object-aware uncertainty score guiding next-best-view (NBV) selection.

A diagonal Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) approximation, updated online via an exponential moving average of squared gradients, ensures tractability. Experiments on Mip-NeRF 360, Light Field, and Tanks & Temples datasets show that OUGS improves object-level reconstruction quality (PSNR +2 dB over FisherRF) while maintaining competitive global performance.


Clarity of Exposition: OK


Quality of References: Good


Technical Soundness and Validation: Average.


Reproducibility: Good


Ethics: None


Explanation of Recommendation: Clear motivation. The paper identifies an important gap—scene-level uncertainty metrics are biased by background clutter—and proposes an object-centric solution.

Physically interpretable uncertainty. Deriving uncertainty from explicit Gaussian parameters is novel and conceptually elegant, improving interpretability compared to weight-space methods.

Natural integration into the 3DGS pipeline. The method builds on existing differentiable splatting frameworks, making it broadly reusable.

Comprehensive experiments. Evaluations span several datasets, with both quantitative and qualitative comparisons to leading NBV methods (ActiveNeRF, Bayes’ Rays, FisherRF, GauSS-MI).

Clear visualizations. Figures 4–6 effectively show convergence curves and correlation between uncertainty maps and reconstruction errors.

3. Weaknesses and Detailed Critique
3.1 Limited conceptual novelty

The paper’s core contribution—combining parameter-level FIM uncertainty with a segmentation mask—is a straightforward hybrid of existing components. FisherRF [ JLD24 ] already propagates Fisher information in 3DGS, and SAM-2 masking is a known strategy. While the integration is neat, it feels evolutionary rather than revolutionary.
The authors should clarify how the mathematical formulation differs fundamentally from simply weighting FisherRF scores by a semantic mask. Can the latter be combined with a mask to work?

3.2 Evaluation ambiguity

Runtime, memory footprint, and scalability with the number of Gaussians are not quantified. No evaluation on multi-object scenes.

3.4 Dependency on external segmentation

The pipeline critically depends on accurate SAM-2 masks. Mask noise or segmentation drift could severely degrade performance, as partially shown in Fig. 7, but no experiments on real imperfect masks or weakly-supervised setups are provided. There should be more examples of object level recontruction and the visualization of the associated soft masks used for construction.

3.5 Presentation issues


Figures 3–5 are dense and could benefit from simpler captions emphasizing what uncertainty visualization reveals beyond prior work. How are these figures related to object-level reconstruction?


3.6 Insufficient comparison to recent work

Recent papers like POp-GS [ WAM et al. 2025 ] and Uncertainty-Aware Normal-Guided GS [ TCZ et al. 2025 ] are mentioned but not compared experimentally. Given their conceptual proximity, excluding them weakens claims of state-of-the-art performance.



-------------------- Review 2 --------------------

Overall Recommendation: 7 (7 - good)
Evaluation Confidence: 4 (4 - Pretty confident, I know this area well)

Summary: This manuscript proposes a method to quantify the uncertainty in a 3D Gaussian splat reconstruction and use it to guide next best view selection. It does so by modeling every parameter in every Gaussian as a normal distribution and measuring the effect of each parameter's covariance in each pixel color through a second-degree Taylor expansion.


Clarity of Exposition: The exposition is very clear. The method is well explained, and the state of the art is well described. The point about implicit methods not being able to produce geometrically meaningful covariances is perhaps repeated a bit too much, especially given it seems at best arguable: a neural network's weights and their distributions result in a distribution over pixel values much in the same way as this work, and one could use those to guide novel-view synthesis in the same way (the benefit here is that 3DGS does not rely on a  network representation, thus removing this need, but that seems more a benefit of 3DGS than of this work).

There seems to be an editing error on (line numbers are missing, likely due to wrong LaTeX template) the bottom left of page 6.

The introduction mentions a "growing number of applications where the primary goal is [...] to capture a specific object of interest". It would be great to add examples of these applications here!


Quality of References: References are adequate and well discussed. The description of POp-GS (which was on arXiv on March 25th, six months before the EG deadline) as "concurrent work" is a stretch, but the authors are correct that no official implementation was available for it (indeed, I am unable to find an official implementation even today), so they should not be penalized for not comparing to it.


Technical Soundness and Validation: Overall, the method is technically correct, although a lot of the statistical formalism seems superfluous: I could be wrong, but it seems to me like once all the approximations are factored in, this entire algorithm amounts to a sensitivity analysis on the loss and pixel values with respect to each of the 3DGS' parameters masked by an externally produced semantic mask. That being said, simplicity is a feature, not a bug: the method seems to work and improve the state of the art for this very important task, so this is not a complaint.

I was, however, left quite confused about the theoretical or philosophical meaning behind the uncertainty being computed. In a multi-view reconstruction setting, there are generally two sources of uncertainty: one related to missing data (epistemic, i.e., regions of an object that have not been imaged yet), and another related to contradicting data (aleatoric, i.e., regions of an object that have been imaged with contradicting information due to calibration or pixel errors).

From my own experience on uncertainty quantification on multi-view reconstruction, it seems to me like any measure of uncertainty that depends on the Gaussians' parameters will not be able to estimate epistemic uncertainty: in regions that have not been imaged, no Gaussians are generated, so the algorithm would return full certainty. However, this seems to me like the most important type of uncertainty one needs to quantify in a next-view-planning setting like the one motivating this paper. The method clearly works quantitatively; therefore, what am I missing?

I would have appreciated a discussion of this, and even more so some toy 2D examples of the produced uncertainty quantities to understand the effect of the different sources. Is it possible that this method shines most when most of the object has been imaged at least once, and one is looking to refine the reconstruction only? (i.e., when one is in the "exploitation" regime of the "exploration/exploitation" process).

I was also surprised by the use of a(n external?) semantic mask for the object. How is this computed, exactly? Further, how is this mask estimated for novel views that have not been seen yet? After introduction and related work sections motivating the importance of object-centric novel view planning, it felt somewhat disingenuous to offload object segmentation in this way.


Reproducibility: I am convinced the work is fully reproducible.


Ethics: N/A


Explanation of Recommendation: This is a very well written paper proposing a novel method for an important tasks that improves or matches the state of the art. I congratulate and thank the authors for their submission. I look forward to reading their response (especially to the "Technical Soundness" section above) and other reviewers' opinions, and there is definitely a chance I have missed something, but I see no reason to reject this work.


-------------------- Review 3 --------------------

Overall Recommendation: 6 (6 - acceptable)
Evaluation Confidence: 4 (4 - Pretty confident, I know this area well)

Summary: This paper introduces OUGS, a novel framework for object-centric active view selection in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). Unlike prior scene-level uncertainty methods, OUGS explicitly models uncertainty from the physical parameters of 3D Gaussians (position, scale, rotation, color) and integrates semantic segmentation masks to disentangle object and background uncertainty. The uncertainty is propagated via a Jacobian formulation and approximated efficiently using a diagonal Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) with an EMA update. Experiments on Mip-NeRF360, Tanks & Temples, and Light Field (LF) datasets show consistent improvements in object-level reconstruction quality and convergence speed over baselines such as FisherRF, GauSS-MI, and ActiveNeRF.


Clarity of Exposition: 1. Efficiency and scalability: Can the authors report training and view-selection runtimes relative to GauSS-MI? How does the EMA-based FIM update scale with large numbers of Gaussians (>1M)?
2. Mask quality and robustness: How sensitive is OUGS to segmentation errors in more cluttered, real-world scenes where SAM2 may fail? Could weakly-supervised or self-supervised masking be integrated?
3. Extensibility: Could the framework be generalized to multi-object scenarios by aggregating or weighting multiple uncertainty maps? How might the diagonal FIM assumption be relaxed, e.g., via block-diagonal or low-rank updates?


Quality of References: GooGood


Technical Soundness and Validation: Good.


Reproducibility: Good.


Ethics:


Explanation of Recommendation: Strengths
1. Clear motivation and contribution: The paper identifies a well-defined gap—the lack of object-aware uncertainty modeling in active 3DGS reconstruction—and directly addresses it.
2. Methodological rigor: The uncertainty derivation from explicit Gaussian parameters and propagation via the rendering Jacobian is mathematically sound and interpretable.
3. Comprehensive experiments: Multiple datasets and strong baselines (ActiveNeRF, FisherRF, GauSS-MI) are evaluated under both scene-level and object-level metrics.
4. Strong qualitative analysis: Figures 4–6 effectively illustrate convergence advantages, uncertainty localization, and visual fidelity improvements.
5. Ablation and analysis: The paper provides detailed studies on mask quality, EMA schedule, and FIM diagonal approximation, demonstrating careful validation of design choices.

Weaknesses
1. Marginal global performance gain: While object-level results are strong, scene-level (panoramic) performance shows limited improvement and occasionally falls behind GauSS-MI, suggesting a trade-off between object precision and overall coverage.
2. Dependence on semantic masks: The method assumes access to reliable SAM2 segmentation masks, which limits applicability in unsegmented or real-time capture scenarios.

Simplified FIM approximation: The diagonal assumption ignores inter-parameter correlations within Gaussians, potentially oversimplifying uncertainty interactions in complex scenes.
3. Computational overhead not fully quantified: Although the method is said to be efficient, runtime analysis and resource comparisons against FisherRF or GauSS-MI are missing.
4. Limited discussion of generalization: The method is tuned for single-object settings; the extension to multi-object or dynamic environments remains unexplored.

Overall, this paper presents a technically solid and well-motivated contribution to object-centric uncertainty modeling in 3DGS. The methodology is rigorous and the experiments are comprehensive, clearly validating the proposed framework. However, the dependence on segmentation masks, limited scalability discussion, and marginal scene-level gains weaken its general applicability. The paper would benefit from additional efficiency analysis and exploration of multi-object or unsupervised extensions.

Summary: This work leverages the explicit nature of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) by introducing variance for each parameter to represent uncertainty. This uncertainty is then computed and updated utilizing the discrepancy between the rendered and ground-truth colors. This approach provides a novel framework for active reconstruction and object-centric reconstruction methodologies.


Clarity of Exposition: The exposition lacks clarity, as the methodology section does not effectively align with reader comprehension logic, creating significant potential for misinterpretation. I am uncertain whether my understanding is accurate – specifically, whether the Jacobian matrices for each Gaussian parameter are computed via the render loss to update the parameter covariance matrix. To enhance clarity, the authors should explicitly illustrate the figure 2 for updating this covariance matrix.


Quality of References: This technically justified method is validated by substantial experiments and ablations. It also honestly discusses its limitations at the end.


Technical Soundness and Validation: Yes.


Reproducibility: Yes.


Ethics: no.


Explanation of Recommendation: There seems to be a symbol discrepancy in Figure 3 where the notations may not fully align with those of the Gaussian parameters. Additionally, in the experimental section, the textual descriptions and their corresponding figures are placed far apart, making it cumbersome to read and cross-reference. Figure 2 does not illustrate the process of updating the parameter covariance matrix, which lacks clarity. How significant is the impact of using semantic segmentation masks on the experimental results? In Table 1, were the probability masks also used in the Panoramic evaluation part? What would the results be if probability masks were completely omitted? Would the reconstruction accuracy of the object decrease? If semantic segmentation masks are used, is only one object selected at a time? How would the method handle scenarios with multiple central objects?
